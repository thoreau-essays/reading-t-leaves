If the manuscript of *Walden* bears witness to the book's evolution in Thoreau's mind, it also bears witness to his process of revision. We can gain some insight into the former by [transcribing Thoreau's alterations to the words of *Walden* over many drafts](https://digitalthoreau.org/fluid-text-toc). But a transcript alone won't take us very far with the latter. If we want to understand how Thoreau *made* the work that became *Walden*, we need to attend to his engagement with the manuscript as a material artifact. We need to ask how Thoreau handled the task of making *Walden* by handling his manuscript as a *thing* &mdash; or, to be more precise, a collection of things, of individual leaves repeatedly assembled, disassembled, and reassembled to form draft after draft, leaves often covered with lines, symbols, and notes intended to remind the maker how the pages of one draft should fit into the next.

Physical evidence provides the main support for the hypothesis that HM 924, the manuscript of *Walden* at the Huntington Library, represents seven distinct stages of development in the work's evolution. Thoreau scholar J. Lyndon Shanley arrived at that hypothesis in 1957 after sorting the manuscript's 628 leaves by various material clues. In *The Making of "Walden," With the Text of the First Version*, Shanley explains that, having begun his examination of the *Walden* manuscript by looking at photostats,

<blockquote class="ehw">
<p>When I went to the manuscript itself to check my reading of the photostats, I found the clues that led to the sorting and proper ordering of all its leaves. Many of the first versions of the passages for which I had assembled the variants were on leaves of the same light-blue paper and of the same size, and they had been written in a smaller hand and in a lighter ink than the other versions; in addition, many of these leaves bore the same embossed stationer’s mark: a rose on a stem with leaves and a bud. Furthermore, although the contents of some of these leaves are in passages that are widely separated in the published text of <em>Walden</em>, they formed consecutive passages in the manuscript when the leaves they are on were brought together. In one group of leaves, for example, material that is in “Economy,” 85 and 96 in the published text, made a consecutive passage in the manuscript; and in another group material of the following paragraphs ran consecutively: “Higher Laws,” 7, “Brute Neighbors,” 10, “Spring,” 25, “Brute Neighbors,” 9, ‘Winter Animals,” 13.</p>

<p>I therefore assembled all the leaves of this particular kind in the manuscript, and when I had discovered their proper order, they made a complete picce, save where leaves were missing. When their contents are compared with the other material in the manuscript, it is clear that they constitute the first version of <em>Walden</em> in the manuscript and, without a doubt, the first version that Thoreau wrote.</p>

<p>Since the color and size of paper, ink, and handwriting, and the stationer’s mark had led to the identification of one distinguishable group of leaves, I went on to see if differences in these points in the remaining leaves would identify other distinct groups. They did so. (Shanley, 3&ndash;4)</p>
</blockquote>

The digitization of HM 924 has made it possible to examine the physical evidence of Thoreau's work on the manuscript in even greater detail. The present essay doesn't attempt to do so exhaustively. Its purpose is to provide a starting point for further investigation. We're interested less in the evidence for establishing stages of composition than in the symbols, scribbles, pin-pricks, knife-cuts, and other markings that show Thoreau *at work* in the process of composing.

As Shanley recognized in 1957, not all of the markings on HM 924 are in Thoreau's hand. That's because of how others have handled the manuscript &mdash; in some cases, mishandled it &mdash; since the author's death. 

To consider how Thoreau handled the task of making *Walden* by handling his manuscript, then, it's best to begin by considering what happened to the manuscript after he died.
