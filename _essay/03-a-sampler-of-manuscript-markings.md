At the top of this partial leaf from Version A, we see Thoreau at work on language that in the published *Walden* would form part of "Economy," paragraph 11. As published, it reads as follows:

<blockquote class="ehw"><p>One farmer says to me, <q>You cannot live on vegetable food solely, for it furnishes nothing to make bones with,</q> and so he religiously devotes a part of his day to supplying his system with the raw material of bones; walking all the while he talks behind his oxen, which, with vegetable-made bones, jerk him and his lumbering plough along in spite of every obstacle.</p></blockquote>

Near the top left of the leaf, Thoreau has edited the first part of the sentence in ink.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3200" map-y="3000" map-zoom="3.5" %}

What began as "One says you can't live &hellip;" becomes "One farmer says to me you can't live &hellip;" after Thoreau cancels "says," places a caret after "One," and writes "farmer says to me" above the line.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html %}

There is a penciled "P.17" in the upper left corner of the page, but this is in Sanborn's hand, not Thoreau's. 

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3600" map-y="4500" map-zoom="4" %}

A few lines from the top of the page, Thoreau inserts "he talks" in ink following "all the while," and he uses a curved line &mdash; a standard transposition symbol &mdash; to indicate that the order of words here should be "walking all the while he talks." As he often does throughout the manuscript, Thoreau draws the line in pencil, then draws over it in ink.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="6000" map-y="3000" map-zoom="4" %}

An inked insertion on the next line amends the last part of "whose vegetable-made bones jerk his" to "jerk him &amp; his". On the line below that, Thoreau, in pencil, cancels "through" and replaces it with "in spite of."  

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="5000" map-y="6030" map-zoom="3.5" %}

Still farther down the page, beyond the sentence in question, Thoreau has penciled an instruction to himself&mdash;"omit"&mdash;presumably referring to everything below the penciled line beneath. "Omit" appears elsewhere in the manuscript in Thoreau's hand, as does the opposite instruction, "retain." 

Thoreau's "omit" should not be confused with Sanborn's occasional notation, "omitted," typically referring to material included in the manuscript that Thoreau chose not to include in the published *Walden*.

<img src="https://cdm16003.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/iiif/p16003coll16/100/675,5400,840,1700/full/pct:30/default.jpg" style="width: 50%" />
<p style="font-size: smaller; font-style: italic;">Sanborn's <q>omitted</q> in the left margin of <a href="https://cdm16003.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/collection/p16003coll16/id/100">a leaf in version A</a>.</p>

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3000" map-zoom="3" animate="zoom-out" %}

The penciled line extends from the bottom of the page, in the left margin, all the way across the width of the page. Thoreau frequently encloses text in this way. The text below the line, beginning "Nature and human life," isn't omitted from the published *Walden* but is indeed omitted from this location; Thoreau relocates it within "Economy" to what will become paragraph segment 13a.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-end.html %}

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-map.html tile-path="/assets/tiles/68dir" image-width="8708" image-height="11608" max-zoom="6" min-zoom="2" layout="sidecar" interactive="true" %}

On this page from Version A, we see page numbers inscribed by both Sanborn and Thoreau, a variety of Thoreau's other extra-textual markings, and evidence of two ways that Thoreau held leaves together as he re-arranged them within and across draft versions. In A, these re-arrangements may have been related to his use of the manuscript for lecturing. The text on this page appears in paragraph 85a of the published *Walden*.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html %}

On the top portion of the page, Thoreau has drawn three vertical lines. They may be intended to cancel the underlying text, but it's also possible that they're intended to remind Thoreau that he's carried the underlying text into another location in the draft, or into a subsequent draft. The text here does make it, with revisions, into the published *Walden*.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html %}

In the left margin, holes and knife-cuts in the paper provide evidence that Thoreau held this leaf together with other leaves using pins at one time and string at another.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3100" map-y="2765" map-zoom="3" %}

Beginning six lines from the top of the page, Thoreau has used curved lines in ink, together with the numbers "2" and "1" in the left margin, to transpose two bits of text.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3800" %}

Thoreau's original inditing reads, "They had to my senses a fragrance like manna – a real cereal fruit which I ripened – which I kept in as long as possible by wrapping them in cloths." He then cancels "manna," adding above it "the fruits." He adds "It was" in front of "a real cereal," then cancels "It was," adding "They were" in pencil above a caret. And he adds a penciled ampersand in front of "They had to my senses." 

You can press and drag in the image to see more of what's to the right.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html %}

With these revisions and the transposition, the result is, "They were a real cereal fruit which I ripened &amp; they had to my senses a fragrance like the fruits which I kept in as long as possible by wrapping them in cloths." This is very close to the wording of the sentence in the published *Walden*.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="4800" map-y="4000" map-zoom="4" %}

The wavy penciled line following the end of the sentence is likely intended to draw together the end of this paragraph and the beginning of the next, collapsing them into a single paragraph.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="2400" %}

Thoreau continues the penciled line on the next line of text, effectively canceling the paragraph indentation in front of "I made." 

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3500" map-y="9000" map-zoom="3" %}

Near the bottom of the page, Thoreau again collapses two paragraphs into one with a penciled line.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="1350" map-y="902" map-zoom="4" %}

Near the top left of the page, Sanborn has written, in blue pencil, "P. 99."

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="7000" map-y="902" %}

In the upper right corner, Thoreau has written "49" in pencil, then replaced it with "99." Thoreau numbered many of the recto leaves in A. The previous recto is numbered "47" and the succeeding one "51." His re-numbering here may be related to his use of the manuscript as a lecture text.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-end.html %}

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-map.html tile-path="/assets/tiles/573dir" image-width="8626" image-height="11231" max-zoom="6" min-zoom="2" layout="sidecar" interactive="true" %}

The top portion of this leaf from Thoreau's D draft of *Walden* contains the end of what would become paragraph 3 of the chapter "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For." As published, the sentence that begins at the end of the second line reads,

<blockquote class="ehw"><p>Why, the owner does not know it for many years when a poet has put his farm in rhyme, the most admirable kind of invisible fence, has fairly impounded it, milked it, skimmed it, and got all the cream, and left the farmer only the skimmed milk.</p></blockquote>

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html %}

A single, vertical penciled line through most of the page is likely one of Thoreau's use marks, indicating that he's incorporated the text beneath elsewhere. The penciled "P 131" and "P. 132" in the left margin are in Sanborn's hand.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3600" map-y="1600" map-zoom="3.5" %}

"The most admirable kind of invisible fence" has been inserted, first in pencil, then in ink, above a caret, and "impounded it" and "milked it" have been added as well.

The next paragraph, after some revision, begins, "All that I could say then with respect to farming &hellip;"

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3600" map-y="4000" map-zoom="4" %}

Between the paragraphs, Thoreau has inserted, in pencil, "V S The real". Thoreau frequently uses the letter "V" as an abbreviation for Latin "vide" ("see") and the letter "S" as an abbreviation for "scrap".

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-end.html %}

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-map.html tile-path="/assets/tiles/684dir" image-width="12000" image-height="15556" max-zoom="6" min-zoom="1" layout="sidecar" interactive="true" %}

This leaf is from draft E. It contains a fair copy of the passage in D, adopting the revised wording we saw above. 

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="9000" map-y="5200" map-zoom="3.5" %}

Here again Thoreau has penciled "VS The real" between the two paragraphs.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-end.html %}

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-map.html tile-path="/assets/tiles/931dir" image-width="12000" image-height="15556" max-zoom="6" min-zoom="1" layout="sidecar" interactive="true" %}

This leaf in G is likely the one to which Thoreau's cross-references in D and E are pointing.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="4200" map-y="1200" map-zoom="3.5" %}

Thoreau has underscored the word "real" on the G leaf.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html %}

Thoreau's two cross-references on leaves from drafts D and E to a scrap in G vividly illustrate a point Shanley makes about G: it consists of short passages that "Thoreau fitted into the earlier groups"&mdash;mostly into F, but also into B, C, D, and E (32). Thoreau likely composed the leaves of G during February and March, 1854. Basing the Hollowell farm passage on an entry in his Journal dated February 3, 1854, he fitted it just where his cross-references indicate on the D and E leaves: between the paragraph about the poet who puts a farm "in rhyme" and the one beginning, "All that I could say, then, with respect to farming &hellip;" 

Another way to understand this relationship between drafts is to describe G as not merely the February–March, 1854 leaves, but rather the combination of those leaves and various predecessor leaves, held together in whatever order, and in whatever manner, made sense to Thoreau at the time&mdash;an order and manner now beyond reconstruction.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-end.html %}

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-map.html tile-path="/assets/tiles/97dir" image-width="8708" image-height="11608" max-zoom="6" min-zoom="2" layout="sidecar" interactive="true" %}

Thoreau occasionally uses an orange grease pencil to mark up the manuscript. Near the center of this leaf, he uses it to indicate revisions to one of *Walden*'s most famous passages.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3000" map-y="7500" map-zoom="3" %}

In the published *Walden*, the passage from the chapter "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For," begins:

<blockquote class="ehw"><p>Time is but the stream I go a-fishing in. I drink at it; but while I drink I see the sandy bottom and detect how shallow it is. Its thin current slides away, but eternity remains.</p></blockquote>

Here, Thoreau has surrounded his draft of the passage on three sides and drawn a large "X" through it, all in pencil.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="1200" map-zoom="4" %}

But in the left margin, he's penciled the word "retain." It's possible that he at first intended to cancel the passage, then changed his mind. More likely, however, the penciled lines indicate his intention to reposition this material, which on the present leaf follows material that would become paragraph 17 of "Where I Lived." In the published *Walden*, the passage becomes the chapter's final (23rd) paragraph. 

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="4200" map-y="5500" %}

Thoreau's cross-reference–"V back The Preacher" refers to material that would form the last part of paragraph 20: "The preacher, instead of vexing the ears of drowsy farmers &hellip;" Thoreau effectively repositions the "Time is but the stream passage" not by re-copying it, but by instructing himself to insert additional material ahead of it. The material that ultimately forms paragraphs 18–22 of "Where I Lived" had earlier been grouped with material that would end up in the chapter "Sounds."

(We discuss this repositioning, together with an [earlier A draft](https://cdm16003.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/collection/p16003coll16/id/93) of the "Time is but the stream" passage, in our essay ["The Nick of Time, Improved"](https://digitalthoreau.org/the-nick-of-time-improved/).)

While we're here, note the penciled lines Thoreau has drawn on either side of the word "drink."

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="4500" map-y="6000" map-zoom="5" %}

He's also penciled "retain" above the word "drink".

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="5200" map-zoom="3" %}

At one point in the revision, process, Thoreau's thought seems to be to strike "I drink at it" and begin a new sentence with the word "But." He uses a pencil to write an upper-case "B" beside the lower-case "b" in ink. It's possible that he also considered, separately, subtituting another word for "drink," judging from those penciled lines on either side of it.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="3400" %}

"But while I drink at it I see" seems to be the revised wording Thoreau intends at first.

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-x="4000" map-zoom="4" %}

However, having inserted "at" in pencil, above a penciled, caret, he cancels it. 

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-zoom="3" %}

Here's a transcript of the revisions in question:

<blockquote class="ehw" style="color: black;"><p>Time is but the stream I go<br /> a-fishing in. <s style="color: orange;"><span style="color: black;">I <span style="color: gray;">|</span> drink <span style="color: gray;">|</span> at it</span></s>, <span style="color: gray;">B</span>but<br /> while I drink <span style="color: gray;"><s>at it</s></span> I see the sandy bot-<br />tom and detect how shallow it is.<br /><span style="color: orange;">(</span><s style="color: orange;"><span style="color: black;">Its thin current slides away, but</span></s><br /><s style="color: orange;"><span style="color: black;">eternity remains</span></s><span style="color: orange;">)</span>.</p></blockquote>

(The parentheses in orange grease pencil surrounding the final sentence are accompanied by penciled parentheses.)

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html map-zoom="2" %}

In the end, as is always the case when trying to understand Thoreau's revision process, we're left with some uncertainty. Does the word "retain" above the word "drink" apply to that word alone or the whole sentence to which it belongs? Similarly, does "retain" in the left margin, beside the penciled line, refer to the words covered in orange grease pencil or the entire lower portion of the page?

{% include essay/feature/scrolly-step.html %}

What's certain, however, is the heightened sense of revision as a *process* that comes from attending to the physical appearance of Thoreau's manuscript, and the greater intimacy with Thoreau the reviser that comes from familiarizing ourselves with his characteristic markings.
